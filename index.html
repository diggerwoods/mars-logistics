THE FUNDAMENTAL CONSTRAINT

You cannot reorder. You cannot expedite. You cannot call your supplier.

Every supply chain on Earth operates on the assumption that if you run out of something, you can get more. You can expedite a shipment, call a backup supplier, or drive to a store. On Mars, your next resupply is 26 months away at minimum, 6-9 months in transit, and 100 metric tons per launch. If you miscalculate your food supply, people die. This is demand planning at its most consequential.

// LEAN ANALYSIS

The Mars Supply Chain Value Stream

Every supply chain has a value stream. Mars is no different — it's just longer, slower, and less forgiving. Here's where the waste accumulates and where the leverage points are.

STAGE 1
Earth Manufacturing
Design, build, test, pack
WASTE POINTS
Over-engineering for Earth conditions that don't apply on Mars. Packaging weight that consumes precious payload capacity. Long lead times on custom components with no backup supplier.
CI LEVER
Standardize components. Design for Mars-first. Every kilogram saved is $2,000+ in launch cost.
↓
STAGE 2
Launch Window
26-month cycle, fixed
WASTE POINTS
Missing a launch window is catastrophic — 26 months of delay with no expedite option. Late manufacturing, late testing, or late integration means waiting two years. This is the hardest constraint in the entire system.
CI LEVER
Build to the window, not to perfection. Ship what's ready. Iterate on Mars. The perfect is the enemy of the alive.
↓
STAGE 3
Transit
6-9 months, no intervention
WASTE POINTS
Cargo damage from radiation, thermal cycling, and microgravity. No ability to inspect, reroute, or recover. Communication delay means problems are discovered long after they occur. Zero visibility into cargo condition.
CI LEVER
IoT sensors on every container. Autonomous condition monitoring. Design packaging to survive worst-case, not average-case.
↓
STAGE 4
Surface Distribution
Unload, store, allocate
WASTE POINTS
No existing infrastructure. Every warehouse, vehicle, and storage system has to be built first. Dust contamination of equipment. Pressurized storage requirements for most consumables. Human labor is the scarcest resource.
CI LEVER
Automate everything possible. Human time is too valuable for manual logistics. Robots unload. Humans decide.
↓
STAGE 5
Consumption and Feedback
Use, monitor, reorder
THE OPPORTUNITY
Real-time consumption data fed back into the next launch window manifest. Predictive demand modeling based on actual usage patterns. The feedback loop is 26 months long — so the data quality has to be exceptional.
CI LEVER
This is where continuous improvement lives. Every mission teaches you something. Build the learning loop into the system from day one.
// THE OPEX ANGLE

What a program manager sees that an engineer might miss.

The rocket gets you there. Operations keeps you alive. Here's how the frameworks I use every day apply to the hardest supply chain problem ever attempted.

📈
Demand Forecasting

On Earth, a bad forecast means a stockout or excess inventory. On Mars, a bad forecast means people die. The 26-month lead time means you're forecasting consumption 3+ years in advance. Every assumption has to be stress-tested against worst-case scenarios, not average ones.

🔒
Constraint Theory

The Theory of Constraints says every system has one bottleneck that limits throughput. On Mars, the constraint is launch capacity — 100 metric tons per Starship, once every 26 months. Every other optimization is secondary until you've maximized what goes on each rocket.

♻
Lean and Waste Elimination

Every kilogram of packaging is a kilogram of food you didn't ship. Every redundant system is weight that could have been medicine. Lean thinking on Mars isn't about efficiency — it's about survival. The 8 wastes of Lean become life-or-death decisions.

📋
Change Management

A colony of 500 people in an isolated, high-stress environment with no ability to leave is the ultimate change management challenge. Process changes can't be mandated — they have to be adopted. The human system is as important as the supply system.

🔄
Continuous Improvement Loops

The feedback loop on Mars is 26 months long. That means you have to build learning into the system from day one — capturing consumption data, failure modes, and process deviations so the next mission manifest is better than the last. Kaizen at interplanetary scale.

🎯
Risk Management

On Earth, risk management is about probability and impact. On Mars, any single-point failure in a critical system is potentially fatal. The risk framework has to be built around resilience and redundancy, not just mitigation. Every critical supply needs a backup supply.

// MY TAKE

The biggest operational risk in current Mars planning.

One opinion, clearly stated. This is a thought experiment — but it's grounded in how I think about operational risk in complex programs.

THE RISK I'D FLAG FIRST

We're optimizing for the launch. We should be optimizing for the feedback loop.

Most Mars planning focuses on what goes on the rocket. That's the right first question. But the second question — what do we learn from each mission that makes the next one better — gets far less attention. And that's where the real operational risk lives.

The 26-month launch window means your feedback loop is extraordinarily long. If you don't build systematic data collection and learning into the first mission, you're flying the second mission on assumptions. And the third. By the time you have enough data to optimize, you've already made expensive — potentially fatal — mistakes.

What I'd do: treat the first mission as a data collection exercise as much as a survival exercise. Every consumption rate, every equipment failure, every process deviation gets captured and fed back into the next manifest. The goal isn't just to survive Mission 1. It's to make Mission 2 dramatically better.
